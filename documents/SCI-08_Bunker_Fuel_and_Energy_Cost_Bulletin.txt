1. WHY FUEL MATTERS TO FREIGHT COSTS
------------------------------------
Fuel is the largest variable cost of operating a ship. Carriers pass fuel cost changes to shippers through bunker adjustment factors (BAF) and emergency bunker surcharges, so bunker prices feed into the total landed cost of imports with a lag of weeks to months. Longer routings, such as the Cape of Good Hope detour, increase fuel burn per voyage.
Main grades: VLSFO (very low sulphur fuel oil, 0.50% sulphur) is the standard bunker grade; HSFO (high sulphur fuel oil) is cheaper and used by ships fitted with exhaust scrubbers; LSMGO (low sulphur marine gasoil) is a distillate used in emission control areas.

3. TREND SUMMARY
----------------
Singapore VLSFO, the world's largest bunkering hub benchmark, rose four months in a row from a $725/mt monthly average in June to $926/mt in October 2026, the highest since July 2022, then eased to $867/mt in November to date (to 18 November). HSFO climbed every month from $550/mt in July to $883/mt in October. Singapore LSMGO peaked at a $1,604/mt monthly average in March 2026 and stood at $1,201/mt in November. Bunker availability in Singapore has been tight, with suppliers' loading schedules heavily booked through November and VLSFO lead times of 10-15 days advised in early October.
Brent crude: about $72/bbl before the war (27 February 2026), above $115 in late April, Q2 2026 average $102.93/bbl (EIA). In November 2026 Brent has been supported by attacks on tankers in and around the Strait of Hormuz and strikes on Russian refineries, and capped by rising US crude inventories. Saudi Arabia resumed crude loadings at its Red Sea terminal of Yanbu in early October 2026.

Reading the table: the Red Sea crisis shifted oil flows from Bab el-Mandeb and Suez to the Cape of Good Hope in 2024. Hormuz carried about a fifth of world oil consumption before the 2026 war, which is why its closure moved crude and bunker prices so sharply.
